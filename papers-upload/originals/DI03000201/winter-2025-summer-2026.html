Q1(a)Define: (i) Mechanical Advantage (ii) Velocity Ratio (iii) Efficiency.3 marks⌄

Model answer · 3 marks

  1. Mechanical advantage (MA): ratio of load lifted to applied effort, MA = W/P.
  2. Velocity ratio (VR): ratio of the distance moved by the effort to the distance moved by the load in the same time, VR = sP/sW.
  3. Efficiency: ratio of useful output work to input work. For a lifting machine, η = MA/VR; as a percentage, η% = (MA/VR)×100.

For an ordinary real machine 0 < η < 1 because some input work is lost to friction and other effects.

Q2(a)State and explain Lami’s theorem.3 marks⌄

Model answer · 3 marks

If a particle is in equilibrium under three coplanar, concurrent, non-collinear forces P, Q and R, each force is proportional to the sine of the angle between the other two:

P/sin α = Q/sin β = R/sin γ

Here α is the included angle between Q and R (opposite P), and similarly for β and γ. The theorem follows by constructing the closed triangle of the three force vectors and applying the sine rule. It applies only to three-force equilibrium, not an arbitrary force system.

Q2(b)Explain the characteristics of a force.4 marks⌄

Model answer · 4 marks

A force is a vector action on a body. To specify it fully, state:

  1. Magnitude and unit (newton, N).
  2. Direction and sense (which way along its direction line).
  3. Line of action (the straight line on which it acts).
  4. Point of application on the body.

A force can change translation, produce rotation about a point/axis, or deform a body. Its turning effect about O is the moment MO = F × perpendicular distance to the line of action.

Q2(c)A single-purchase crab has an 800 mm handle, a 200 mm load-drum diameter, a 20-tooth pinion and a 100-tooth spur wheel. A 150 N effort lifts 4.5 kN. Find VR, MA, efficiency and say whether the machine is reversible.7 marks⌄

Model answer · 7 marks

Let handle radius rh = 800 mm and drum radius rd = 100 mm. In one handle revolution the pinion turns the spur wheel/load drum through 20/100 revolution.

VR = (2πrh/2πrd)(Tspur/Tpinion)
= (800/100)(100/20) = 40
MA = W/P = 4500/150 = 30
η = MA/VR = 30/40 = 0.75 = 75%

By the usual lifting-machine criterion, η > 50% indicates a reversible machine: on removing effort, the descending load can overcome friction and drive the mechanism backwards. This is the ideal textbook criterion; an actual machine also depends on its brake/ratchet and operating conditions.

Q2(a) — ORDefine (i) Elasticity (ii) Ductility (iii) Malleability.3 marks⌄

Model answer · 3 marks

  1. Elasticity: ability of a material to recover its original size and shape after unloading, within its elastic range.
  2. Ductility: ability to undergo appreciable plastic deformation in tension before fracture; it is commonly indicated by elongation or reduction in area.
  3. Malleability: ability to undergo plastic shaping under compressive action, for example rolling or hammering into sheet.

Elasticity concerns recovery; ductility and malleability concern plastic forming in different loading modes.

Q2(b) — ORExplain the conditions of equilibrium of coplanar concurrent forces.4 marks⌄

Model answer · 4 marks

For coplanar concurrent forces, choose perpendicular in-plane axes x and y through the common point. Static equilibrium requires the vector sum to be zero:

ΣFx = 0,   ΣFy = 0

Equivalently, the force polygon closes. Since all lines of action pass through the common point, each force has zero moment about that point, so a separate moment equation is not needed for this concurrent system. For non-concurrent coplanar forces, also require ΣM = 0 about a point.

Q2(c) — ORA simple lifting machine raises 60 kN with 3 kN effort and 100 kN with 4 kN effort; VR = 50. (i) Derive the law of machine. (ii) Find effort for 200 kN. (iii) Find maximum efficiency.7 marks⌄

Model answer · 7 marks

Take the usual linear law P = mW + C, where P and W are in kN. Use the two observations:

m = (4−3)/(100−60) = 0.025
C = 3−0.025(60) = 1.5 kN
Law: P = 0.025W + 1.5
At W = 200 kN: P = 0.025(200)+1.5 = 6.5 kN
MAmax = 1/m = 40
ηmax = MAmax/VR = 40/50 = 0.80 = 80%

Maximum efficiency is the limiting value as load becomes large in the linear machine-law model; the constant no-load effort C then contributes a diminishing fraction of total effort.

Q3(a)Define (i) Centroid (ii) Centre of gravity.3 marks⌄

Model answer · 3 marks

  1. Centroid: geometric mean position of a line, area or volume. For a plane area, x̄ = ΣAx/ΣA and ȳ = ΣAy/ΣA.
  2. Centre of gravity (CG): point through which the resultant gravitational force (the body's total weight) acts.

For a uniform homogeneous lamina in a uniform gravitational field, the centroid and CG coincide.

Q3(b)State and explain the polygon law of forces.4 marks⌄

Model answer · 4 marks

If several concurrent forces acting on a point are represented in magnitude and direction by successive sides of a polygon taken head-to-tail, the closing side drawn from the tail of the first vector to the head of the last represents their resultant in magnitude and direction.

If the force polygon closes, its closing side has zero length and the resultant is zero: the forces are in equilibrium. Draw every vector to a consistent scale and preserve its direction when placing it head-to-tail.

Q3(c)Find the centroid of an Indian Standard Angle section 90 × 60 × 8 mm, with the longer leg vertical and the shorter leg as the base.7 marks⌄

Model answer · 7 marks

Take origin at the outside lower-left corner, x to the right and y upward. Use vertical rectangle 8×90, horizontal rectangle 60×8, and subtract their 8×8 overlap once.

A = 720 + 480 − 64 = 1136 mm²
x̄ = [720(4)+480(30)−64(4)]/1136 = 14.986 mm
ȳ = [720(45)+480(4)−64(4)]/1136 = 29.986 mm

Therefore the centroid is approximately 14.99 mm from the outer vertical edge and 29.99 mm above the outside base. Uniform thickness and square-corner geometry are assumed.

Q4(a)Enlist various tests carried out on metals.3 marks⌄

Model answer · 3 marks

Common tests include:

  • Mechanical: tensile, compression, bend, torsion, impact (Izod/Charpy), hardness (Brinell/Rockwell/Vickers), fatigue and creep tests.
  • Physical/chemical: density, electrical/thermal properties, composition and corrosion tests.
  • Non-destructive examination: visual, dye-penetrant, magnetic-particle, ultrasonic and radiographic tests.

The selected test depends on the property or discontinuity being assessed; these methods are not interchangeable.

Q4(b)Draw and explain the stress–strain curve with all salient points for a tension test on a mild-steel bar.4 marks⌄

Model answer · 4 marks

Plot engineering stress σ = load/original area against engineering strain ε = extension/original gauge length. The initial straight portion obeys Hooke’s law; its slope is Young’s modulus E. The proportional limit ends linearity; the elastic limit is the last stress with no permanent set after unloading. Mild steel may show an upper yield point, a drop to a lower yield point and a yield plateau. During strain hardening stress rises to the ultimate tensile strength (UTS). Necking then localises deformation and engineering stress falls to fracture.

Yield features vary with steel grade and test conditions; true stress differs from engineering stress after the area starts to shrink.

Q4(c)A 1.5 m long, 25 mm diameter mild-steel bar carries 100 kN axially in tension. For E = 2×10⁵ N/mm², find stress, strain, elongation and final length.7 marks⌄

Model answer · 7 marks

A = πd²/4 = π(25²)/4 = 490.874 mm²
σ = P/A = 100,000/490.874 = 203.72 N/mm² (MPa)

Lfinal = 1500+1.528 = 1501.528 mm ≈ 1.50153 m

Q4(a) — ORDefine (i) Resilience (ii) Proof Resilience (iii) Modulus of Resilience.3 marks⌄

Model answer · 3 marks

  1. Resilience: ability of a material to absorb and release energy within its elastic range.
  2. Proof resilience: maximum strain energy stored by the whole body up to the adopted elastic limit.
  3. Modulus of resilience: proof resilience per unit volume. For linear elasticity to limiting elastic stress σe, ur = σe²/(2E).

The limiting stress must be stated or understood for the material/test convention.

Q4(c) — ORA 25 mm diameter steel bar is fixed between walls 4 m apart at 80°C; temperature falls to 10°C. Find stress and force for (i) non-yielding walls and (ii) walls yielding by 2 mm. E = 2×10⁵ N/mm², α = 12×10⁻⁶/°C.7 marks⌄

Model answer · 7 marks

Cooling is ΔT = 10−80 = −70°C. The free change would be αLΔT = 12×10⁻⁶(4000)(−70) = −3.36 mm (contraction). Area A = π(25²)/4 = 490.874 mm².

(i) Rigid/non-yielding supports: total length change is zero, so σ = −EαΔT = +168 MPa tensile. Force = σA = 168(490.874) = 82.47 kN tensile.

(ii) Supports yield toward one another by 2 mm: take support spacing change δs = −2 mm. Compatibility gives σ = E(δs/L − αΔT) = 2×10⁵(−2/4000 + 0.00084) = +68 MPa tensile. Force = 68(490.874) = 33.38 kN tensile.

Assumption / convention: Interprets ‘walls yield by 2 mm’ as a 2 mm reduction in wall spacing, i.e. movement that permits part of the bar's cooling contraction. If displacement direction differs, its signed value must be changed in the compatibility equation.

Q5(a)State and explain the parallel-axis theorem.3 marks⌄

Model answer · 3 marks

For a plane area A, the second moment of area about an axis parallel to a centroidal axis and at perpendicular distance d is:

I = IG + Ad²

Here IG is about the parallel axis through the area centroid. It follows by expanding ∫(y+d)²dA; the first-moment term vanishes because the reference axis passes through the centroid. For mass moment of inertia the analogous relation is I = IG + Md².

Q5(b)Define (i) Linear strain (ii) Lateral strain (iii) Poisson’s ratio (iv) Volumetric strain.4 marks⌄

Model answer · 4 marks

  1. Linear (longitudinal) strain: axial change in length divided by original length, εL = ΔL/L.
  2. Lateral strain: transverse change in a dimension divided by its original value, e.g. εlat = Δd/d.
  3. Poisson’s ratio: ν = −εlat/εL in uniaxial elastic loading; the minus sign makes ν positive for the usual tensile lateral contraction.
  4. Volumetric strain: change in volume divided by original volume, εv = ΔV/V. For small strains, εv ≈ εx+εy+εz.
Q5(c)(i) Two 100 kN forces act at 60°; find the resultant magnitude and direction. (ii) A 20 kN body hangs from 50 cm and 120 cm strings attached to hooks 130 cm apart; the strings meet at right angles at the body. Find both tensions.7 marks⌄

Q5(a) — ORDefine polar moment of inertia and write its equation for a circular shaft.3 marks⌄

Model answer · 3 marks

The polar moment of area J about an axis normal to the cross-section through a point is the area integral of squared radial distance: J = ∫r²dA = Ix+Iy for perpendicular in-plane axes through that point. For circular shafts:

Solid: J = πd⁴/32
Hollow: J = π(D⁴−d⁴)/32

These are area moments with units of length⁴; they govern the elementary elastic torsion of circular shafts.

Q5(b) — ORWrite the assumptions made in the theory of torsion.4 marks⌄

Model answer · 4 marks

  • The shaft is straight, prismatic, circular and of uniform material/section over the considered length.
  • Material is homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic (Hooke's law in shear); stresses remain within the elastic range.
  • Twist is small and uniform; the shaft carries torque about its longitudinal axis, away from local end effects.
  • Plane cross-sections normal to the axis remain plane and circular after twist; radial lines remain straight, with no warping.

These assumptions support the elementary circular-shaft relation τ/r = T/J = Gθ/L; do not use it directly for arbitrary non-circular shafts.

Q1(a)Define: centroid, centre of gravity and axis of symmetry.3 marks⌄

Q1(c)State the classification of a force system and explain each type.7 marks⌄

Model answer · 7 marks

Classify first by whether all lines of action lie in one plane, then by their geometric arrangement.

SystemDescription / example
CollinearAll forces act along the same straight line; they may reinforce or oppose one another.
Coplanar concurrentLines of action lie in one plane and meet at one point (e.g. forces at a pin joint).
Coplanar parallelLines of action lie in one plane and are parallel; same- or opposite-sense parallel forces are possible.
Coplanar general (non-concurrent, non-parallel)All lines lie in one plane but are neither all concurrent nor all parallel; e.g. general loading of a rigid plate.
Non-coplanar concurrentForces meet at one point but do not all lie in one plane (3-D joint).
Non-coplanar parallelParallel lines of action are distributed in space, not in one common plane.
Non-coplanar general3-D forces are neither all concurrent nor all parallel; a full force-and-moment balance is needed.

Collinear forces are a special concurrent case. In three dimensions equilibrium generally requires ΣFx = ΣFy = ΣFz = 0 and ΣMx = ΣMy = ΣMz = 0.

Q2(b)State and explain the parallelogram law of forces.4 marks⌄

Model answer · 4 marks

If two forces acting simultaneously at a point are represented in magnitude and direction by two adjacent sides of a parallelogram drawn from that point, their resultant is represented in magnitude and direction by the diagonal through the common point.

For forces P and Q with included angle θ, resolve along and perpendicular to P:

R = √(P²+Q²+2PQ cosθ)
tan α = Q sinθ/(P+Q cosθ)

Here α is the angle from P toward Q. The signs/components should be checked if θ is obtuse.

Q2(b) — ORState the triangle law and polygon law of forces.4 marks⌄

Model answer · 4 marks

Triangle law: if two concurrent forces are represented by two sides of a triangle taken in order, their resultant is represented by the third side taken from the tail of the first to the head of the second.

Polygon law: if any number of concurrent forces are represented by successive sides of a polygon taken head-to-tail, the closing side from the first tail to the last head represents the resultant. A closed force polygon means zero resultant and equilibrium.

Q2(c) — ORState and explain Lami’s theorem and define a free-body diagram.7 marks⌄

Model answer · 7 marks

Lami’s theorem: for a particle in equilibrium under three coplanar concurrent, non-collinear forces P, Q and R,

P/sinα = Q/sinβ = R/sinγ

where α, β and γ are the angles between the other two forces (the angle opposite each force). It follows from the sine rule applied to the closed force triangle.

Free-body diagram (FBD): a sketch of one isolated body showing all external applied forces, its weight, and the support/contact reactions or couples acting on it. Replace each support by its allowed reaction components; do not include internal forces unless the body is deliberately cut.

Main question set — answer all printed parts
Q3(a)State all types of stresses and explain any two.3 marks⌄

Model answer · 3 marks

Common engineering stresses are tensile (direct normal), compressive (direct normal), shear, bending/flexural, torsional and bearing/crushing stress; thermal restraint can also create normal stress.

  • Tensile stress: axial pulling force per resisting area, σ = P/A; it tends to lengthen a member.
  • Shear stress: tangential force per shear area, τ = V/A (average); it tends to make adjacent material layers slide.

For non-uniform stress distributions, use the appropriate local section formula rather than assuming P/A everywhere.

Q3(a) — ORDefine thermal stress and thermal strain, and write the thermal-stress equations when (1) ends do not yield (2) ends yield.3 marks⌄

Model answer · 3 marks

Thermal strain: free strain caused by a uniform temperature change, εth = αΔT; free length change is αLΔT. Thermal stress is normal stress produced when expansion/contraction is restrained.

For a signed support-separation change δs, compatibility gives εtotal = δs/L = σ/E + αΔT, hence:

General yielding supports: σ = E(δs/L − αΔT)
Rigid / non-yielding ends (δs=0): σ = −EαΔT

Thus heating under full restraint gives compression and cooling gives tension. If supports yield/move, enter their signed movement; the stress magnitude is E|αΔT−δs/L|.

Q3(b) — ORDefine strain energy. Explain different types of loading on a body that result in strain energy, with examples.4 marks⌄

Model answer · 4 marks

Strain energy is the energy stored in a body as it deforms elastically. It can arise from axial tension/compression, shear, bending, torsion or restrained thermal deformation.

For a linearly elastic axial member, U = ½Pδ = P²L/(2AE) = σ²V/(2E). Loading history changes the maximum response:

  • Gradually applied load: load rises from zero to P; work/energy stored U = ½Pδst.
  • Suddenly applied load: constant P is applied with zero drop height and no damping; ideal maximum extension/stress is twice the static value, δmax=2δst, σmax=2P/A.
  • Impact or falling load: a weight falling through height h does additional work while the member deflects. For an ideal axial bar, W(h+δ)=AEδ²/(2L); impact stress depends on h and stiffness, not simply a universal factor of two.

Real damping, member inertia, contact effects and yielding may change these ideal results.

Q3(c) — ORA 50 mm × 50 mm steel bar, 1 m long, carries 300 kN axial tension. For E = 2×10⁵ N/mm² and μ = 0.25, find changes in length, width, thickness and volume.7 marks⌄

Model answer · 7 marks

Assume uniform uniaxial elastic tension, free lateral contraction and small-strain relations. Initial volume V = 50×50×1000 = 2.5×10⁶ mm³.

σ = 300,000/(50×50) = 120 MPa
εL = σ/E = 120/(2×10⁵) = 0.000600
ΔL = εL(1000) = +0.600 mm
εlat = −μεL = −0.25(0.0006) = −0.000150
Δwidth = Δthickness = −0.000150(50) = −0.00750 mm each

So the bar lengthens, both transverse dimensions contract, and the first-order volume change is an increase of about 750 mm³.

Q4(a)Define (1) Moment of Inertia (2) Section Modulus (3) Radius of Gyration.3 marks⌄

Model answer · 3 marks

  1. Second moment of area (area moment of inertia): I = ∫y²dA about a stated axis; it measures the geometric distribution of area from that axis (units L⁴).
  2. Elastic section modulus: Z = I/c, where c is the distance from the centroidal bending axis to the relevant extreme fibre; units L³.
  3. Radius of gyration: k = √(I/A), the distance at which the whole area could be concentrated to give the same I; I = Ak², units L.

This section property I is not the mass moment of inertia used in rotational dynamics.

Q4(b)State and explain the parallel-axis theorem.4 marks⌄

Model answer · 4 marks

The second moment of a plane area A about any axis parallel to its centroidal axis is:

I = IG + Ad²

d is the perpendicular distance between the axes. For example, for x-coordinates y measured from the centroidal axis, the parallel axis is at y+d:

I = ∫(y+d)²dA = ∫y²dA + 2d∫y dA + d²∫dA
= IG + Ad², because ∫y dA = 0 at the centroid.

Use the theorem only with the matching area and axis orientation; it is not IG−Ad².

Q4(a) — ORDefine (1) Elasticity (2) Plasticity (3) Toughness.3 marks⌄

Model answer · 3 marks

  1. Elasticity: ability to recover original dimensions after unloading, within the elastic limit.
  2. Plasticity: ability to undergo permanent deformation without fracture after the applied stress exceeds the elastic range.
  3. Toughness: ability to absorb energy before fracture under stated conditions; for a tensile specimen, it is related to the area under the complete stress–strain curve per unit volume.

Toughness is not identical to hardness, strength or resilience.

Q4(b) — ORExplain the classification of engineering materials according to physical properties.4 marks⌄

Model answer · 4 marks

A useful engineering classification groups materials by broad physical behaviour and composition:

  • Metals and alloys: generally good thermal/electrical conductors, relatively strong and formable; ferrous (steels, cast irons) and non-ferrous (Al, Cu, Ti alloys).
  • Polymers/organic non-metals: usually low density and poor electrical conductors; thermoplastics, thermosets and elastomers differ in heat response and forming behaviour.
  • Ceramics and glasses/inorganic non-metals: often hard, heat- and corrosion-resistant, electrically insulating, but comparatively brittle in tension.
  • Composites: combine two or more constituents (e.g. fibre-reinforced polymer) to tailor strength, stiffness, density or directional properties; semiconductors are a special class with controllable electrical conductivity.

These families have characteristic trends, not universal grade-level values; actual selection needs tested material data.

Q5(b)Write the torsion equation, explain every term, and state the assumptions of the theory of torsion.4 marks⌄

Model answer · 4 marks

For a uniform circular shaft in elastic torsion:

T/J = τ/r = Gθ/L
  • T: applied torque; J: polar second moment of area; τ: shear stress at radius r.
  • G: shear modulus; θ: angle of twist in radians over length L; τmax occurs at outer radius R, so τmax/R = T/J.

Assumptions: straight, uniform circular shaft; homogeneous, isotropic, linearly elastic material; small, uniform twist; torque about the longitudinal axis; plane cross-sections remain plane and circular and radial lines remain straight (no warping); local end effects are neglected. The relation is not directly valid for non-circular shafts.

Q5(c)A single-purchase winch crab has a 60 cm handle, 20-tooth pinion, 100-tooth spur wheel and 18 cm load drum. It lifts 2400 N with 80 N effort and 4800 N with 160 N effort. Find the machine law and maximum efficiency.7 marks⌄

Model answer · 7 marks

Let P = mW+C, with P and W in newtons. From the two observations:

m = (160−80)/(4800−2400) = 0.0333333
C = 80−m(2400) = 0
Law of machine: P = W/30

Velocity ratio (handle radius 60 cm; drum radius 9 cm):

VR = (60/9)(100/20) = 33.333
MAmax = 1/m = 30
ηmax = MAmax/VR = 30/33.333 = 90%

Here C=0 in the fitted ideal linear law; real friction/no-load effort need not be zero.

Q5(a) — ORDefine (1) Compound Machine (2) Velocity Ratio (3) Ideal Machine.3 marks⌄

Model answer · 3 marks

  1. Compound machine: a combination of two or more simple machines arranged so one stage's motion/output drives the next; overall VR is the product of stage VRs when the stages are independent in series.
  2. Velocity ratio: distance moved by effort divided by distance moved by load in the same time.
  3. Ideal machine: a loss-free machine with 100% efficiency, so MA = VR and input work equals output work.
Q5(b) — ORExplain in detail the law of a machine.4 marks⌄

Model answer · 4 marks

For a given machine under a stated operating condition, effort P is approximately a linear function of load W:

P = mW + C
  • m is the slope of the effort–load graph and is the incremental effort per unit increase in load.
  • C is the intercept, the effort predicted at zero load; it represents friction/no-load effort in the idealised model.
  • Two measured (W,P) pairs determine m = (P₂−P₁)/(W₂−W₁), then C = P₁−mW₁.

MA = W/(mW+C) and η = W/[VR(mW+C)]. As W increases, the model's limiting maximum efficiency is ηmax = 1/(m·VR). The law is an empirical operating-range approximation, not a universal law independent of condition.

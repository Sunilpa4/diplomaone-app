Main question set — answer all printed parts
Q1(c)Write definition of heat and work and also write five points of equality between them.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

Heat (Q): energy transferred across a system boundary solely because of a temperature difference. Heat flows spontaneously from higher to lower temperature.

Work (W): energy transferred across the boundary by an organised interaction other than heat, such as a force acting through a displacement or a rotating shaft crossing a control surface.

Five similarities:

  1. Both are modes of energy transfer across the boundary, not properties stored in the system.
  2. Both occur only during a process; a system is described as having energy, not “heat” or “work”.
  3. Both are boundary phenomena and their amount depends on the process path.
  4. Both are measured in joules (or kJ); their rates are watts (or kW).
  5. Both can produce the same change in a system’s energy and can be converted from one transfer mode to the other (for example, paddle-wheel work raises water’s internal energy).

With the convention used here, heat into the system and work done by the system are positive; keep the sign convention consistent in balances.

Q1(c) — ORExplain Zeroth law of thermodynamics with its applicability using neat sketch.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

Statement: If two systems A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other.

Thermal equilibrium means no net heat transfer when the bodies are placed in thermal contact; their temperatures are equal. If TA=TC and TB=TC, then TA=TB.

Applicability — thermometry: Let C be a calibrated thermometer. Bring it to thermal equilibrium with A and record its stable reading; repeat with B. Equal readings imply A and B have the same temperature, and when placed together they exchange no net heat. This provides the logical basis for measuring temperature and constructing a temperature scale. A thermometer must be allowed to settle and should not appreciably disturb the body.

Q2(a)What is a perpetual motion machine of the first kind (PMM-1)? Write relative statement of corollary of the First Law of Thermodynamics.3 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 3 marks

A PMM-1 is a hypothetical device that delivers net work continuously without an equivalent energy input, or creates energy from nothing. It violates the first law and cannot exist.

Corollary: Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred or transformed. For a closed system, ΔE = Q − W (with Q positive in and W positive out); over a complete cycle ΔE=0, so net heat supplied = net work delivered. All energy forms and transfers must be included.

Q2(b)Write the simplified form of the steady flow energy equation for a steam turbine and explain the meaning of each term.4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

For one inlet and one outlet at steady flow, using heat into the turbine and shaft work out as positive:

q − ws,out = (h2−h1) + (V2²−V1²)/2 + g(z2−z1)

For an approximately adiabatic turbine with negligible kinetic- and potential-energy changes:

ws,out ≈ h1 − h2;   Pout ≈ ṁ(h1−h2)

Here q is heat transfer per unit mass (kJ/kg), ws,out is shaft work output per unit mass (kJ/kg), h is specific enthalpy (kJ/kg), V is velocity (m/s), z is elevation (m), g is gravitational acceleration (m/s²), and ṁ is mass-flow rate (kg/s). If heat loss or changes in V or z matter, retain the corresponding terms; do not assume every turbine is adiabatic.

Q2(c)In a gas turbine, air enters with velocity 200 m/s and enthalpy 6699 kJ/kg at 4.5 kg/s. At the outlet, velocity is 150 m/s and enthalpy is 5460 kJ/kg. If 50.4 kJ/kg of heat is rejected to the surroundings, determine the turbine power developed.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

Given: ṁ=4.5 kg/s; h₁=6699, h₂=5460 kJ/kg; V₁=200, V₂=150 m/s; q=−50.4 kJ/kg (heat rejected). Assume steady, one-inlet/one-outlet flow and negligible elevation change.

  1. SFEE per kg, with shaft work positive out: wout = q + (h₁−h₂) + (V₁²−V₂²)/2000. The divisor 2000 converts a velocity-squared difference in m²/s² to kJ/kg.
  2. Enthalpy contribution: 6699−5460=1239 kJ/kg.
  3. Kinetic-energy contribution: (200²−150²)/2000 = 8.75 kJ/kg.
  4. wout=−50.4+1239+8.75=1197.35 kJ/kg.
  5. Pout=ṁwout=4.5(1197.35)=5388.1 kW ≈ 5.388 MW.

The heat loss reduces output by 50.4 kJ/kg; it is entered once as negative q.

Q2(a) — ORWrite the four conditions for steady flow.3 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 3 marks

  1. Mass and energy stored within the control volume do not change with time.
  2. At every fixed location, flow properties (pressure, temperature, velocity, etc.) are time-independent.
  3. Mass-flow rates and heat/work interaction rates are steady in time.
  4. Mass conservation is satisfied: for a single inlet and outlet, ṁin=ṁout; for several ports, total inflow equals total outflow.

Steady does not mean inlet and outlet properties are equal; they may differ while each remains constant in time.

Q3(a)Write the formulas for (1) COP of a refrigerator (2) COP of a heat pump (3) Entropy.3 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 3 marks

COPR = QL/Win
COPHP = QH/Win = COPR + 1

For a cyclic refrigerator/heat pump, QH=QL+Win, and heat quantities are positive magnitudes. For entropy, the defining reversible differential relation is:

dS = δQrev/T   (or ds=δqrev/T per unit mass)

Entropy S is a property; units are J/K (specific entropy: J/(kg·K)). For an irreversible process, entropy generation is non-negative; do not generally replace δQ by δQrev with the actual irreversible heat transfer.

Q3(b)Write Kelvin–Planck’s statement of the Second Law of Thermodynamics and explain it with the help of a diagram.4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

Statement: No device operating in a cycle can have as its sole effect the absorption of heat from a single thermal reservoir and the conversion of that heat completely into work.

A cyclic heat engine must reject some heat to a colder sink; thus its thermal efficiency is less than 100%. This does not forbid a non-cyclic process from converting energy while the system changes state, nor does it forbid work being dissipated as internal energy. The words cyclic and sole effect are essential.

Q3(c)What is irreversibility? Write its types. List the factors that make a process irreversible.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

Irreversibility is the inability to reverse a real process so that both the system and surroundings are restored exactly to their initial states with no other effect. Irreversibility is associated with entropy generation.

Types: (i) internal irreversibility, caused within the system (viscous flow, friction, mixing, chemical reaction); (ii) external irreversibility, caused by interactions across the boundary or in the surroundings (for example, heat transfer through a finite temperature difference or friction at a boundary). A real process can involve both.

Common causes/factors:

  • Mechanical friction, viscous shear and turbulence
  • Heat transfer across a finite temperature difference
  • Unrestrained/free expansion and pressure equalisation through finite differences
  • Mixing of different substances or streams
  • Electrical resistance and Joule heating
  • Inelastic deformation, impact and hysteresis
  • Combustion or other chemical reactions away from equilibrium

These effects generate entropy; a reversible idealisation requires their absence and quasi-static transfer through infinitesimal driving differences.

Q3(b) — ORState the Clausius statement of the Second Law of Thermodynamics and explain it with a diagram.4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

Statement: No device operating in a cycle can have as its sole effect the transfer of heat from a colder body to a hotter body.

A refrigerator can move heat from cold to hot only by receiving work (or having another compensating effect). The electrical work supplied to an ordinary refrigerator is why it does not violate Clausius. In the ideal prohibited case shown, no work is supplied and the sole effect would be cold-to-hot heat transfer.

Q3(c) — ORWrite the names of any five irreversible processes and describe the fast compression–expansion process with a diagram.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

Examples (any five): free expansion of a gas; heat transfer across a finite temperature difference; flow with friction/turbulence; mixing of different gases; electrical resistance heating; inelastic impact; combustion.

Fast compression–expansion: A piston compressing gas rapidly creates finite pressure differences and strong non-equilibrium gradients. The compression is not a succession of equilibrium states; work must be supplied against the gas pressure and dissipative effects. If the gas is then allowed to expand rapidly back, friction, turbulence and other entropy-generating effects prevent complete recovery of that input work. The return path differs from the compression path, so the net cycle requires work input.

The schematic P–v loop shows the compression branch above the expansion return: area between the branches represents net work input for the indicated cycle. Rapid does not by itself prove adiabatic; heat transfer may be neglected only if the duration and insulation justify it. The sketch is qualitative because an actual fast non-equilibrium process has no unique equilibrium P–v path at every instant.

Q4(a)State Boyle’s law and explain it.3 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 3 marks

For a fixed mass of gas at constant absolute temperature, the pressure is inversely proportional to volume:

p ∝ 1/V;   pV = constant;   p₁V₁=p₂V₂

Reducing volume by half doubles pressure under these conditions. The P–V graph is a rectangular hyperbola. Boyle’s law is an ideal-gas relation (or a useful low-density approximation for a real gas).

Q4(b)State the characteristic equation of a gas and indicate the units of each term.4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

The characteristic (ideal-gas) equation for a fixed mass is:

pV = mRT   (equivalently, pv=RT)
  • p: absolute pressure, Pa; V: total volume, m³.
  • m: mass, kg; T: absolute temperature, K.
  • R: specific gas constant, J/(kg·K); v=V/m: specific volume, m³/kg.

Both sides have units of energy: Pa·m³ = J = kg·[J/(kg·K)]·K. In common thermodynamics units, p in kPa, V in m³ and R in kJ/(kg·K) also give kJ because 1 kPa·m³=1 kJ. For molar quantities, pV=nRuT, where n is mol (or kmol) and Ru is J/(mol·K) (or kJ/(kmol·K)). Use absolute pressure and kelvin.

Q4(c)1 kg of air at 1.5 bar absolute pressure and 30°C passes through a constant-pressure process until its volume triples. Determine (1) change in internal energy (2) change in enthalpy. Take Cv=0.714 and Cp=1.005 kJ/(kg·K).7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

Given/assumption: m=1 kg of ideal gas (air), p constant, V₂=3V₁, T₁=30°C=303.15 K. At constant pressure for a fixed mass, V/T is constant.

  1. T₂/T₁=V₂/V₁=3, so T₂=909.45 K and ΔT=606.30 K.
  2. For ideal-gas air with the stated constant specific heats: ΔU=mCvΔT=1(0.714)(606.30)=432.90 kJ.
  3. ΔH=mCpΔT=1(1.005)(606.30)=609.33 kJ.

Both changes are positive because the gas temperature rises. The absolute starting pressure is not needed once the constant-pressure volume ratio is specified.

Q4(b) — ORDraw the throttling process on P–V and T–S diagrams.4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

For an insulated, steady throttling valve with no shaft work and negligible changes in kinetic and potential energy, the SFEE gives h₁≈h₂. Pressure falls, and the process is irreversible with entropy generation. The diagrams mark equilibrium inlet and outlet states; the dashed connector is only a visual guide, not a quasistatic path.

Q4(c) — ORWrite the definitions of specific heat at constant pressure and specific heat at constant volume, and prove Cp−Cv=R.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

Definitions: specific heat at constant volume Cv is the heat required per unit mass per kelvin for a small temperature rise at constant volume; for an ideal gas Cv=(∂u/∂T)v. Specific heat at constant pressure Cp is similarly defined at constant pressure; Cp=(∂h/∂T)p. Units: kJ/(kg·K).

Proof for an ideal gas:

  1. Specific enthalpy is h=u+pv.
  2. For a fixed-composition ideal gas, pv=RT; hence h=u+RT.
  3. Differentiate with respect to temperature: dh/ dT = du/dT + R, with R constant for the gas.
  4. Using the ideal-gas definitions, Cp=Cv+R.
Cp−Cv=R

The relation is for an ideal gas. Cp and Cv may vary with temperature; their difference still equals the gas constant at a given state.

Q5(c)A Diesel cycle has compression ratio 16. At the beginning of compression, pressure is 9.81 N/cm² and temperature is 20°C. Maximum temperature is 1430°C. Determine thermal efficiency. Take γ=1.4.7 marks⌄

  1. Isentropic compression: T₂=T₁rγ−1=293.15(160.4)=888.66 K.
  2. At constant-pressure heat addition, the cut-off ratio is rc=V₃/V₂=T₃/T₂=1703.15/888.66=1.9165.
  3. For a Diesel cycle, ηth=1−(rcγ−1)/[γrγ−1(rc−1)].
  4. Substitution gives ηth=0.61794, therefore ηth≈61.79%.

Pressure conversion: 9.81 N/cm²=98.1 kPa. It sets the pressure level but cancels from the ideal-cycle efficiency calculation; the supplied maximum and initial temperatures and compression ratio determine the needed ratios.

Q5(a) — ORState the limitations of the Carnot cycle.3 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 3 marks

  • Perfectly reversible operation requires no friction, turbulence, pressure drops or other dissipative effects, which cannot be achieved exactly in a real engine.
  • Isothermal heat addition and rejection require finite heat transfer; at useful finite power this entails a temperature difference and therefore irreversibility. In the reversible limit it is very slow.
  • The two-phase/compression or expansion arrangements and exact isothermal processes can be impractical for an engineering working fluid and device.
  • Consequently, Carnot is a maximum-efficiency benchmark between two reservoirs, not a practical cycle delivering finite power at its ideal efficiency.
Q5(b) — ORWrite the characteristics and applications of the Otto cycle.4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

Characteristics: ideal spark-ignition (constant-volume heat-addition) cycle; four internally reversible processes: (1–2) isentropic compression, (2–3) constant-volume heat addition, (3–4) isentropic expansion, (4–1) constant-volume heat rejection. Its air-standard efficiency is ηOtto=1−1/rγ−1, so in the ideal model it depends on compression ratio r and γ.

Applications/model use: air-standard analysis of petrol/gasoline spark-ignition engines, including small SI engines and some gas-engine models. It is an idealisation; real combustion and heat transfer are not instantaneous constant-volume processes.

Q1(b)Write short note of thermodynamic equilibrium.4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

A system is in thermodynamic equilibrium when it has no unbalanced driving potential and its macroscopic properties do not change with time. The complete condition includes:

  • Thermal equilibrium: uniform temperature; no net heat flow within the system.
  • Mechanical equilibrium: no unbalanced pressure or force causing motion or deformation.
  • Chemical equilibrium: no net chemical reaction or species diffusion.
  • Phase equilibrium: no net transfer of matter between phases.

All applicable forms must hold together. A system in equilibrium can be described by state properties; local uniformity is assumed for the simple single-state model.

Main question set — answer all printed parts
Q1(c)Explain Zeroth law of thermodynamics with an example.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

Statement: If two systems A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with one another.

Let A and B each be separately brought into thermal contact with a thermometer C and allowed to settle. If both give the same stable reading, TA=TC and TB=TC, so TA=TB. If A and B are then placed in contact, there is no net heat transfer between them.

Example/applicability: A thermometer calibrated in degrees Celsius or kelvin measures a hot-water bath. An object and water bath at the same thermometer reading are in thermal equilibrium, subject to measurement uncertainty and negligible thermometer disturbance. The law makes temperature comparison and thermometry meaningful.

Main question set — answer all printed parts
Q2(a)Define enthalpy and write its unit in S.I. system.3 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 3 marks

Enthalpy is the thermodynamic property defined by:

H = U + pV   (total);   h = u + pv   (per unit mass)

It groups internal energy with the pressure/flow-work contribution needed to move fluid across a control surface. SI unit of specific enthalpy h is J/kg (commonly kJ/kg); total enthalpy H is J. Enthalpy is a property, not heat.

Q2(b)Explain Joule's experiment with neat sketch.4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

Joule used an insulated vessel containing water and a paddle wheel driven by a falling mass. The falling mass descends through a measured distance, turning the paddle via a cord and pulley. Paddle friction stirs the water, converting organised mechanical work into microscopic internal energy; the water temperature rises.

  1. Measure initial water temperature and mass.
  2. Allow a known falling mass to turn the paddle under controlled conditions.
  3. Measure the temperature rise after it settles.
  4. Compare mechanical work input with the corrected increase in water/apparatus internal energy.

For a well-insulated vessel, Q≈0 and the shaft work is input (work by the water is negative), so ΔU≈Win. This demonstrates equivalence of work and heat as energy transfers; it does not imply that heat is a stored substance.

Q2(c)List the assumptions for the steady flow energy equation and apply SFEE for a nozzle and turbine with sketch.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

Control-volume assumptions for the usual SFEE: (1) steady operation, so stored mass/energy and conditions at each fixed point do not vary with time; (2) mass conservation, with a single inlet/outlet mass rate equal; (3) inlet/outlet states are represented by uniform section-average properties; (4) changes in kinetic/potential energy or heat/work are neglected only where explicitly stated; chemical/nuclear energy changes are omitted unless relevant.

For one inlet and one outlet, heat q positive in and shaft work ws,out positive out:

q − ws,out = (h₂−h₁)+(V₂²−V₁²)/2+g(z₂−z₁)

Nozzle: stationary passage; ordinarily q≈0, ws=0 and Δz≈0. Thus h₁+V₁²/2=h₂+V₂²/2 (use consistent J/kg units); enthalpy drop raises jet speed. In kJ/kg, V²/2 becomes V²/2000.

Turbine: flowing fluid expands and turns a rotor; shaft work leaves. For q≈0 and negligible ΔKE, ΔPE: ws,out≈h₁−h₂ and Pout≈ṁ(h₁−h₂). If heat, velocity or elevation changes matter, keep those terms instead of dropping them.

Q2(a) — ORState and explain law of conservation of energy.3 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 3 marks

Law: Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transferred across a boundary or transformed from one form to another.

For a closed system, with heat into the system and work done by it positive:

Q − W = ΔU + ΔKE + ΔPE

The left side is net energy transferred; the right side is the change in stored energy. In a complete cycle the state returns to its start, so ΔE=0 and Qnet=Wnet. This conservation law alone does not determine process direction; the second law does.

Q2(b) — ORWrite four conditions for steady flow.4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

  1. Mass within the control volume remains constant; there is no accumulation.
  2. At any fixed location, pressure, temperature, velocity and other flow properties are time-independent.
  3. Mass-flow, heat-transfer and shaft-work rates are constant in time.
  4. Continuity is obeyed: total mass inflow equals total mass outflow; for a single inlet/outlet, ṁ₁=ṁ₂.

Properties at inlet and outlet need not be equal; steady means they do not drift with time at their respective locations.

Q2(c) — ORGas turbine: air enters at 210 m/s and 6700 kJ/kg, ṁ=4.5 kg/s; exits at 150 m/s and 5480 kJ/kg. Heat loss is 50.4 kJ/kg. Find power developed.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

Given: h₁=6700, h₂=5480 kJ/kg; V₁=210, V₂=150 m/s; ṁ=4.5 kg/s; q=−50.4 kJ/kg. Assume steady single-inlet/outlet flow and negligible elevation change.

  1. Use SFEE with turbine shaft output positive: wout=q+(h₁−h₂)+(V₁²−V₂²)/2000.
  2. Δh contribution=6700−5480=1220 kJ/kg.
  3. Δke contribution=(210²−150²)/2000=(44100−22500)/2000=10.8 kJ/kg.
  4. wout=−50.4+1220+10.8=1180.4 kJ/kg.
  5. Pout=ṁwout=4.5(1180.4)=5311.8 kW ≈ 5.312 MW.

The printed English says “exists” at the outlet, which is interpreted as “exits” (consistent with the Gujarati line). The specified heat loss is negative heat transfer and is included once.

Q3(a)Define (1) Heat engine (2) Heat source (3) Heat sink.3 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 3 marks

  1. Heat engine: a cyclic device that receives heat from a high-temperature source, delivers net work and rejects remaining heat to a lower-temperature sink.
  2. Heat source: a body/reservoir supplying heat to the working substance; ideally its temperature changes negligibly.
  3. Heat sink: a body/reservoir receiving rejected heat; ideally its temperature changes negligibly.
Q3(b)Write equation of COP for refrigerator and heat pump.4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

For a refrigerator, the desired effect is cooling the low-temperature space; for a heat pump, it is heating the warm space.

COPR = QL/Win
COPHP = QH/Win

From the cycle energy balance, QH=QL+Win, so COPHP=COPR+1. Use positive heat/work magnitudes. COP is not a thermal efficiency and may exceed one.

Q3(c)A heat engine receives energy at 9500 kJ/min and does work at 6000 kJ/min. Find thermal efficiency and heat-rejection rate.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

Given: Q̇H=9500 kJ/min, Ẇnet=6000 kJ/min. Treat as a cyclic engine at steady operation.

  1. First-law energy balance: Q̇H=Ẇnet+Q̇L.
  2. Q̇L=9500−6000=3500 kJ/min rejected.
  3. ηth=Ẇnet/Q̇H=6000/9500=0.63158=63.16%.

In kW (optional): heat input 158.33 kW, work output 100.00 kW, heat rejection 58.33 kW. Check: 100.00+58.33≈158.33 kW.

Q3(a) — ORWhat is entropy?3 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 3 marks

Entropy S is a thermodynamic property that measures energy dispersal and provides a criterion for process direction. It is defined for a reversible heat transfer by dS=δQrev/T (specific form ds=δqrev/T). Its SI unit is J/K; specific entropy is J/(kg·K).

For an isolated system, entropy cannot decrease: it remains constant for an ideal reversible process and increases when irreversibilities generate entropy.

Q3(b) — ORState 3rd law of thermodynamics.4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

Third law (Planck statement): the entropy of a pure, perfectly crystalline substance approaches zero as its temperature approaches absolute zero, 0 K.

The perfect-crystal condition matters; real disordered solids may have residual entropy. The law supplies a reference for absolute entropy. Its unattainability formulation says that absolute zero cannot be reached by a finite sequence of ordinary cooling operations.

Q3(c) — OROne heat engine works between 160°C and 750°C. Find its maximum possible thermal efficiency.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

Maximum possible efficiency is the reversible Carnot efficiency between the two reservoir temperatures. Convert to kelvin first:

  1. TL=160+273.15=433.15 K; TH=750+273.15=1023.15 K.
  2. ηmax=ηCarnot=1−TL/TH.
  3. ηmax=1−433.15/1023.15=0.57665.

Maximum thermal efficiency ≈57.67%. A real engine operating between the same reservoirs has a lower efficiency. Celsius must not be used in the ratio.

Q4(a)Explain concept of ideal gas. Write characteristic equation for ideal gas.3 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 3 marks

An ideal gas is a model in which molecular volume and intermolecular forces are neglected in the equation of state. It obeys:

pV=mRT   (or pv=RT;   pV=nRuT)

Use absolute pressure and kelvin. In the ideal-gas model, internal energy and enthalpy depend on temperature alone. The approximation is most useful at low density, away from condensation; real gases can deviate at high pressure or near saturation.

Q4(b)Explain (1) specific heat at constant pressure (Cp) (2) specific heat at constant volume (Cv).4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

Specific heat at constant pressure Cp: heat required per unit mass to raise temperature by one kelvin in a constant-pressure process (for an ideal gas, Cp=(∂h/∂T)p).

Specific heat at constant volume Cv: heat required per unit mass per kelvin in a constant-volume process (for an ideal gas, Cv=(∂u/∂T)v).

SI units: J/(kg·K), commonly kJ/(kg·K). For an ideal gas Cp>Cv, because at constant pressure some supplied heat supports expansion work as well as increasing internal energy.

Q4(c)A gas has temperature 300°C, pressure 10 bar, volume 14.5 m³ and mass 32 kg. Find its characteristic gas constant.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

Given/assumption: T=300+273.15=573.15 K; interpret the stated p=10 bar as absolute (the paper does not say gauge), so p=1000 kPa; V=14.5 m³; m=32 kg. Assume the gas follows the ideal-gas equation.

  1. From pV=mRT, solve R=pV/(mT).
  2. R=[(1000 kPa)(14.5 m³)]/[(32 kg)(573.15 K)].
  3. Because 1 kPa·m³=1 kJ, R=14500/18340.8=0.79059 kJ/(kg·K) (≈790.6 J/(kg·K)).

Use absolute temperature and consistent pressure/energy units. The given gas need not be air; the result is its specific characteristic constant under the ideal-gas model.

Q4(a) — ORWrite statement of (1) Boyle's law (2) Charles' law.3 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 3 marks

  1. Boyle’s law: for a fixed mass at constant absolute temperature, pV=constant; pressure varies inversely with volume.
  2. Charles’ law: for a fixed mass at constant pressure, V/T=constant; volume varies directly with absolute temperature.

In both laws, T is in kelvin and p is absolute.

Q4(b) — ORDerive Cp−Cv=R with usual notations.4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

For a fixed-composition ideal gas, h=u+pv and pv=RT per unit mass.

  1. Substitute the equation of state into enthalpy: h=u+RT.
  2. Differentiate with respect to temperature: dh/ dT=du/dT+R, since R is constant for the gas.
  3. For an ideal gas, Cp=(∂h/∂T)p and Cv=(∂u/∂T)v; u and h depend on T only.
  4. Therefore Cp=Cv+R, or Cp−Cv=R.

All terms are specific quantities (kJ/(kg·K)). The proof assumes ideal-gas behaviour.

Q4(c) — ORAn ideal gas is at 32°C, 2 bar and 1 m³. It is compressed to 16 bar and 0.3 m³. Find its final temperature.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

For fixed gas mass and constant characteristic R, pV/T is constant. Use absolute temperatures and pressure ratios:

  1. T₁=32+273.15=305.15 K.
  2. T₂=T₁(p₂V₂)/(p₁V₁)=305.15[(16 bar)(0.3 m³)]/[(2 bar)(1 m³)].
  3. Pressure/volume ratio=(16×0.3)/(2×1)=2.4.
  4. T₂=305.15(2.4)=732.36 K.
  5. In Celsius, t₂=732.36−273.15=459.21°C.

Assume the mass is unchanged and the ideal-gas model applies. The stated pressures are used as absolute; if they were gauge pressures, atmospheric pressure would first have to be added.

Q5(a)What is air-standard efficiency?3 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 3 marks

Air-standard efficiency is the thermal efficiency of an idealised air-standard power cycle:

ηas = Wnet/Qin = 1−Qout/Qin

The working fluid is treated as a fixed mass of air in a closed cycle; actual combustion and exhaust are replaced by external heat addition and heat rejection, and the air is commonly modelled as an ideal gas. It is a cycle-model efficiency, not an actual engine brake efficiency.

Q5(b)Explain Carnot cycle with P–V and T–S diagram. State two applications of Carnot cycle.4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

The reversible Carnot heat-engine cycle has four processes:

  1. 1→2: reversible isothermal expansion at TH, absorbing heat QH.
  2. 2→3: reversible adiabatic (isentropic) expansion from TH to TL.
  3. 3→4: reversible isothermal compression at TL, rejecting heat QL.
  4. 4→1: reversible adiabatic (isentropic) compression back to TH.

On T–s, the isotherms are horizontal and the isentropes vertical; the enclosed area is net work. On P–V, the cycle is a closed clockwise engine loop.

Applications: (1) establishes the maximum possible heat-engine efficiency between two reservoirs, ηC=1−TL/TH, to benchmark real engines; (2) provides the reversible upper-limit COP for refrigeration and heat-pump systems when the cycle is reversed. It is a theoretical standard, not a practical finite-power engine.

Q5(c)A gas turbine works on the Brayton cycle with pressure ratio 6. Find air-standard efficiency. Take γ=1.4.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

ηBrayton = 1 − 1/rp(γ−1)/γ
  1. Exponent (γ−1)/γ=(1.4−1)/1.4=0.285714.
  2. 60.285714=1.66851.
  3. η=1−1/1.66851=0.400663.

Air-standard efficiency ≈40.07%. This assumes ideal-gas properties, no pressure losses and ideal isentropic compressor/turbine processes.

Q5(b) — ORState two applications of Brayton cycle.4 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 4 marks

Q5(c) — ORA Carnot engine between 650 K and 310 K produces 150 kJ of work. Find (a) thermal efficiency (b) heat added during the process.7 marks⌄

Worked study answer · 7 marks

Given: TH=650 K, TL=310 K, Wnet=150 kJ per cycle. Temperatures are already absolute.

  1. Reversible Carnot efficiency: η=1−TL/TH=1−310/650=0.523077=52.31%.
  2. η=Wnet/QH, hence QH=Wnet/η=150/0.523077=286.76 kJ per cycle.

The requested heat added is QH, not net heat.

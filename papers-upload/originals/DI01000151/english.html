Q1 · Electronic components

Q1(a) · Define active and passive components with example.3 marks

Question: Define active and passive components with example.

Answer

Active components control current or signals and may provide power gain; they normally require bias or an external supply. Examples: transistor and integrated circuit (IC). A diode is also commonly grouped as an active semiconductor device for rectification and switching, though it does not provide power gain.

Passive components cannot provide power gain; they dissipate, store or release energy. Examples: resistor, capacitor, inductor and transformer.

Q1(b) · What is capacitor? Give its applications.4 marks

Question: What is capacitor? Give its applications.

Answer

A capacitor is a two-terminal passive component that stores electric charge and energy in the electric field between two conductors separated by a dielectric. Its capacitance is C = Q/V, measured in farads (F).

  • Filtering and smoothing rectifier output in power supplies.
  • AC coupling between amplifier stages and DC blocking.
  • Decoupling/bypass of noise on supply lines.
  • Timing, oscillators and tuning circuits.
  • Energy storage, power-factor correction and motor-start circuits.
Q1(c) · Explain resistor colour coding with example.7 marks

Question: Explain resistor colour coding with example.

Answer

The coloured bands are read from the end opposite the tolerance band (usually gold or silver). In a 4-band resistor, bands 1–2 are significant digits, band 3 is the multiplier and band 4 is tolerance. In a 5-band resistor, bands 1–3 are digits, band 4 is multiplier and band 5 is tolerance.

Digit colours: black 0, brown 1, red 2, orange 3, yellow 4, green 5, blue 6, violet 7, grey 8, white 9. Gold multiplier = ×0.1 and ±5%; silver = ×0.01 and ±10%; no tolerance band is typically ±20%.

Example: yellow–violet–red–gold = 47 × 10² Ω = 4.7 kΩ ±5% (acceptable range 4.465 kΩ to 4.935 kΩ).

Q1(c) OR · Explain Faraday’s law of electromagnetic induction with necessary diagram.7 marks

Question: Explain Faraday’s law of electromagnetic induction with necessary diagram.

Answer

First law: whenever the magnetic flux linking a conductor or coil changes, an electromotive force (EMF) is induced. If the circuit is closed, an induced current flows.

Second law: the magnitude of induced EMF is proportional to the rate of change of flux linkage:

e = −N dΦ/dt

Here N is the number of turns and Φ is magnetic flux in webers. The negative sign expresses Lenz’s law: induced EMF/current opposes the change that produces it. Flux may change by relative motion, changing field strength, or changing coil area/orientation.

Q2 · Semiconductors and rectifiers

Q2(a) · What is doping? Give names of trivalent and pentavalent impurities.3 marks

Question: What is doping? Give names of trivalent and pentavalent impurities.

Answer

Doping is the deliberate addition of a very small, controlled amount of impurity to a pure (intrinsic) semiconductor such as silicon or germanium to increase and control its conductivity.

Trivalent (acceptor) impurities: boron (B), aluminium (Al), gallium (Ga), indium (In); they form P-type material. Pentavalent (donor) impurities: phosphorus (P), arsenic (As), antimony (Sb), bismuth (Bi); they form N-type material.

Q2(b) · Explain formation of N-type semiconductor.4 marks

Question: Explain formation of N-type semiconductor.

Answer

Dope pure Si or Ge with a small amount of a pentavalent donor such as phosphorus. Four of its five valence electrons form covalent bonds with neighbouring semiconductor atoms; the fifth is loosely bound and becomes a free electron with little energy. The donor atom becomes a fixed positive ion. Electrons are majority carriers and holes are minority carriers. The material as a whole remains electrically neutral.

Q2(c) · Draw V-I characteristic of P-N junction diode and explain it.7 marks

Question: Draw V-I characteristic of P-N junction diode and explain it.

Answer

The characteristic plots diode current against diode voltage.

  • Forward bias: current is very small below the knee (cut-in) voltage; after the knee it rises rapidly. Typical knee values are about 0.7 V for silicon and 0.3 V for germanium.
  • Reverse bias: only a small reverse saturation current flows over the normal region. At reverse breakdown voltage, current increases sharply; an external resistor must limit it to prevent damage.
  • The curve is nonlinear and the exact values depend on diode material, temperature and device rating.
Q2(a) OR · Explain the concept of free electron and hole.3 marks

Question: Explain the concept of free electron and hole.

Answer

A free electron is a valence electron that gains enough energy to leave its covalent bond and move through the semiconductor; it carries negative charge. The vacant bond is a hole, which behaves as a positive charge carrier. Thermal or light energy can create an electron–hole pair. Under an electric field electrons drift toward the positive terminal and holes toward the negative terminal; conventional current is opposite to electron motion.

Q2(b) OR · Explain formation of P-type semiconductor.4 marks

Question: Explain formation of P-type semiconductor.

Answer

Dope pure Si or Ge with a small amount of a trivalent acceptor such as boron. Each impurity atom forms three covalent bonds; one bond lacks an electron, creating a hole. The impurity accepts an electron and becomes a fixed negative ion. Holes are majority carriers and electrons are minority carriers. The doped crystal remains electrically neutral overall.

Q2(c) OR · Explain half-wave rectifier with necessary circuit diagram and waveforms.7 marks

Question: Explain half-wave rectifier with necessary circuit diagram and waveforms.

Answer

A half-wave rectifier uses one diode in series with a load. During the positive half-cycle (with the shown diode orientation), the diode is forward biased and current flows; the load gets the positive half-wave. During the negative half-cycle the diode is reverse biased, current is nearly zero, and output is approximately zero. The result is pulsating DC, with ripple frequency equal to the AC input frequency. For an ideal diode and sinusoidal input of peak Vm, the average output is VDC = Vm/π and RMS output Vrms = Vm/2.

Q3 · Diodes and semiconductor materials

Q3(a) · Explain diode as a switch.3 marks

Question: Explain diode as a switch.

Answer

A diode acts approximately as an electronic switch:

  • ON / closed: forward bias; after its cut-in voltage it conducts appreciable current. A series resistor limits current.
  • OFF / open: reverse bias; only a small leakage current flows until breakdown.
  • An ideal model treats forward bias as a closed switch and reverse bias as an open switch; a real diode has a forward drop and leakage.

Q3(c) · Describe working, characteristic and applications of varactor diode.7 marks

Question: Describe working, characteristic and applications of varactor diode.

Answer

A varactor (varicap) diode is a P–N junction used in reverse bias. Its depletion layer acts as the dielectric and the P and N regions act as capacitor plates. Increasing reverse voltage widens the depletion layer and reduces junction capacitance (approximately Cj ∝ 1/√(Vbi + VR) for an abrupt junction). Thus it is a voltage-controlled capacitor; its C–V curve falls as reverse voltage rises. It is used for electronic RF tuning, voltage-controlled oscillators (VCOs), radio/TV tuners, PLLs and frequency modulation.

Answer

Varactor: diode symbol with capacitor plates at the junction; it is reverse biased. Photodiode: arrows point toward the junction to indicate incident light; it is normally reverse biased. LED: arrows point outward to show emitted light; it is forward biased.

Q3(b) OR · State the properties of semiconductor material.4 marks

Question: State the properties of semiconductor material.

Answer

Key properties of silicon and germanium semiconductors:

  • Their resistivity/conductivity lies between conductors and insulators.
  • At room temperature they have a small number of free electrons and holes; both are charge carriers.
  • They are usually tetravalent and form covalent bonds; pure Si has a band gap about 1.1 eV and Ge about 0.66 eV at room temperature.
  • They have a negative temperature coefficient: conductivity generally increases as temperature rises.
  • Conductivity can be greatly controlled by doping; light and heat can also create electron–hole pairs.
Q3(c) OR · Describe working, characteristic and applications of LED.7 marks

Question: Describe working, characteristic and applications of LED.

Answer

An LED (light-emitting diode) is a P–N junction diode operated in forward bias. Electrons and holes recombine near the junction and release energy as photons (light); colour depends mainly on semiconductor band gap/material. Its forward current rises rapidly after the knee voltage, so a series resistor or current driver is essential. The forward voltage depends on colour/device (often roughly 1.8–3.3 V); do not exceed current or reverse-voltage ratings. Applications include indicators, seven-segment and other displays, lighting, traffic signals, optical links and status lamps.

Q4(b) · Explain Zener diode as a voltage regulator with necessary diagram.4 marks

Question: Explain Zener diode as a voltage regulator with necessary diagram.

Answer

Connect a Zener diode in reverse breakdown in parallel with the load; connect a series resistor RS between the unregulated input and output node. The Zener holds the load voltage approximately at Vout ≈ VZ. The resistor limits current. By Kirchhoff’s current law:

I = (Vin − VZ)/RS = IZ + IL

For regulation, keep IZ between its specified minimum and maximum and keep Zener power PZ = VZIZ within its rating. The output varies little with input or load while the Zener remains in breakdown.

Q4(c) · Derive the relation between α and β.7 marks

Question: Derive the relation between α and β.

Answer

For a transistor, neglecting leakage current, define common-base current gain α = IC/IE and common-emitter current gain β = IC/IB. Since IE = IC + IB:

α = IC/(IC + IB) = (IC/IB)/(IC/IB + 1) = β/(β + 1)

Therefore β = α/(1 − α) and α = β/(1 + β).

Q4(a) OR · Explain the working of NPN transistor.3 marks

Question: Explain the working of NPN transistor.

Answer

In the active region, the emitter–base junction is forward biased and the collector–base junction is reverse biased. The heavily doped N-type emitter injects electrons into the thin, lightly doped P-type base. A small fraction recombines in the base (giving base current IB); most electrons cross the base and are swept into the collector, producing collector current IC. The relation is IE = IB + IC.

Q4(b) OR · List applications of Zener diode.4 marks

Question: List applications of Zener diode.

Answer

Applications of a Zener diode:

  • Shunt voltage regulator in power supplies.
  • Stable voltage reference for measurement and control circuits.
  • Over-voltage protection and voltage clamping.
  • Wave shaping, clipping and protection of meters/sensitive inputs.
Q4(c) OR · Draw circuit and input and output characteristics for CE amplifier.7 marks

Question: Draw circuit and input and output characteristics for CE amplifier.

Answer

In a common-emitter (CE) circuit the emitter is common to input and output: the input is applied between base and emitter; output is taken between collector and emitter. In the active region it gives useful voltage and current gain and the collector output is 180° out of phase with the input.

  • Input characteristic: IB versus VBE at constant VCE; resembles a forward-biased diode curve.
  • Output characteristic: IC versus VCE for constant IB; shows cutoff, active and saturation regions (and breakdown at excessive VCE).

Answer

The standard NE555 is an 8-pin IC. Pin functions:

Q5(c) · Draw and explain internal block diagram of 555 timer IC.7 marks

Question: Draw and explain internal block diagram of 555 timer IC.

Answer

The IC contains a three-resistor divider (typically three equal 5 kΩ resistors) that creates reference levels near 2/3 VCC and 1/3 VCC. The upper comparator monitors THRESHOLD (pin 6) and resets the S–R latch when it exceeds the upper reference. The lower comparator monitors TRIGGER (pin 2) and sets the latch when it falls below the lower reference. The latch controls the output driver (pin 3) and discharge transistor (pin 7). RESET (pin 4), active LOW, forces reset; CONTROL (pin 5) provides access to the reference level. GND is pin 1 and VCC is pin 8.

Q5(b) OR · Describe application of transistor as a switch.4 marks

Question: Describe application of transistor as a switch.

Answer

An NPN transistor is commonly used as a low-side switch: connect the emitter to ground, the load between +VCC and collector, and drive the base through resistor RB. With input LOW/insufficient base current the transistor is in cutoff, IC ≈ 0 and the load is OFF. With adequate base current it enters saturation, VCE(sat) is small and the load is ON. Choose RB and device ratings for the load; place a flyback diode across an inductive load.

Q5(c) OR · State the various features of 555 timer IC.7 marks

Question: State the various features of 555 timer IC.

Answer

Important features of a typical bipolar NE555 timer:

  • Operates in monostable, astable and bistable applications.
  • Timing is set mainly by external resistor(s) and capacitor(s); useful delays range from microseconds to long intervals.
  • The internal divider and comparators use approximately 1/3 and 2/3 of supply voltage as trigger/threshold levels.
  • Works from a broad supply range (commonly about 4.5–16 V for a bipolar NE555; check the exact manufacturer datasheet).
  • Can source or sink substantial output current; “up to 200 mA” is a device/rating limit, not a recommended continuous design point.
  • Low cost, easy to use and requires few external components; CMOS 555 variants use less supply current.
